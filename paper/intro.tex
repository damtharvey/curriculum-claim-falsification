\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An assessment item arrives with a claim about what answering it requires, and this paper asks whether that claim holds at the level of requirement: whether a solver that lacks the tagged competency can still pass under the published scoring rule. Utah Core and the Common Core State Standards (CCSS) print a standard code. The Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) prints knowing, applying, or reasoning~\cite{iea2009timss2011frameworks}. A passing score is then used as evidence of that competency: in accountability, in curriculum evaluation, and in research that treats tagged items as operationalizations of skills. If a pass does not require the tagged competency, those uses are reading the wrong quantity. The testing standards treat that score use as a claim that must be validated for the intended interpretation~\cite{aera2014standards}.

That inference is an argument, not a definition~\cite{Kane_1992,Kane_2013,Messick_1995}. It can be wrong even when the publisher's tag is sincere. The item may be answerable from a surface cue, from the choices without the stem, or from a formula that never binds symbols to the situation the standard names.

This paper searches for those rival explanations as deficient solver policies. Write $C$ for the tagged competency, $T$ for the tagged items of a cell, and $R$ for the published scoring rule. An \emph{assessment bypass} is a policy $S$ that lacks $C$ yet passes: $S \not\models C$ but $\operatorname{Pass}(S,T,R)=1$. A deficient policy is defined by explicit restrictions on the operations or on the information it receives, so what it lacks is a fact about its specification, not about its internals. The restrictions are of two kinds. Capability-restricted policies (item-writing cues, test-taking strategies, substitution of the options into the printed equation) lack the tagged operation because it is not in their instruction set. Information-restricted policies (a language model shown the question with every given quantity replaced by a placeholder, which we call masked-stem) have the operations and lack the inputs. A bypass by either kind, found without seeing the published key, is a \emph{witness} that the pass does not require the part of the competency the policy was denied. A search that finds no bypass does not certify the tag beyond the policies that were tried. We do not estimate whether students learned the skill, whether humans use the shortcuts, or which curriculum is better. We never say students do not learn~$X$.

A tag can be read as making one of five progressively stronger claims (Table~\ref{tab:claim-ladder}). Alignment tools, which match item text to a standard, address Mention and Opportunity. This paper tests Requirement: does passing require the tagged competency. A bypass refutes Requirement on that cell. Discrimination and retention need student responses and are not tested here.

Results are pass rates of named deficient policies on cells (an authority-by-tag group of items), not a table of items by operation. The pass rate of a deficient family on a cell is a vulnerability measure, meaningful only relative to that documented family, and never a probability that a student lacks the competency. TIMSS items keep their knowing, applying, and reasoning tags; we do not assign CCSS codes to TIMSS items.

Our contributions are the object, the bar, the census, and what the census found.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The assessment bypass as a one-sided test of a tag, with two restriction kinds and no student responses.} We turn the claim that an item requires a competency into a falsifiable statement about solver policies: a bypass is a policy that lacks the tagged competency, by an explicit restriction on its operations or on the information it receives, never sees the published key, and passes under the published scoring rule. Capability-restricted policies (item-writing cues, test-taking strategies, substitution of the options into the printed equation) and information-restricted policies (language models shown the question with every given quantity withheld) are the same object under the two restriction kinds, and each bypass refutes Requirement on its cell for the part of the competency its policy was denied. The test uses no student data and never yields a claim that students failed to learn. Validity theory states the argument; cognitive diagnosis and test-wiseness research estimate students or list cues. Neither scores this object (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
\item \textbf{A pre-registered witness bar and family discipline.} A witness must fire on at least 10 items with a lower 95\% confidence bound above format chance, adjusted for option elimination; searched and fitted programs must also beat the 95th percentile of random programs of matched complexity. Programs are hashed before scoring. Catalog rules, adaptive strategies, and partial-input language models are three families, each Holm-corrected on its own, so a row that beats chance in one cell is never billed as a family-wise discovery. Because the PDF text layer of a released form often differs from the printed page, every claimed row must also clear its bar on the item as printed: the stem and options are re-read from the page image, and for the language-model rows a second independent transcription must agree with the first before the item enters the claimed population. Before any null is read, the same searcher must recover published shallow-cue rates on public benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{A census of tagged public-release mathematics.} We assemble 2{,}405 keyed items from nine authorities, each tag quoted from its authority, including the last TIMSS cycle whose items, keys, and domain tags are public together. Channels are defined by the information and operations a program lacks: surface cues, adaptive test-taking strategies, substitution of the options into the printed equation read from the page image, and language models that see the question with every given quantity withheld.
\item \textbf{What the census found.} The item-writing catalog yields no family-wise discovery; one rule, pick the second-smallest numeric option, beats chance on two cells (Ontario's Education Quality and Accountability Office (EQAO) grade~6 and New York Regents geometry) on options re-read from the page, and awaits replication. The one strategy that survived correction failed a powered replication. Language models in two model families, Qwen2.5 and Phi-4, recover New York Regents Algebra~I and Algebra~II keys with the given quantities withheld, on items re-read from the page and verified by a second transcription, and the recovery survives a change of every option value with the option form kept, on all three scorers and both cells, so memorized items are not the whole effect. Two item-level codings of what each Algebra~I item asks agree with each other moderately and with the publisher's tag barely; on the items both coders mark as requiring operation on the givens, the two Qwen scorers clear the bar and Phi-4 does not. Those bypasses are information-restricted: on the matched items a pass does not require computing on the given quantities, and whether it requires the tagged operation is left open. Substituting the options into the equation read from the page image answers a small subset of Algebra~I. TIMSS knowing, applying, and reasoning stay null at about 50 to 80 public items per domain, a power-limited non-refutation, with the same searcher validated on benchmarks with documented cues (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

This census uses public-release items. TIMSS 2011 is the last cycle whose item texts, keys, scoring guides, domain tags, and percent correct are public together, with no permission process. From 2015, the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) keeps most items secured; restricted-use grants typically cap example items and forbid the comparisons this census needs. Recency of showable items comes from released state and provincial tests, not from a later TIMSS cycle.
