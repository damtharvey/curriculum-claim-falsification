\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An assessment item arrives with a claim about what answering it requires. Utah Core and the Common Core State Standards (CCSS) print a standard code. The Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) prints knowing, applying, or reasoning~\cite{iea2009timss2011frameworks}. A passing score is then used as evidence of that competency: in accountability, in curriculum evaluation, and in research that treats tagged items as operationalizations of skills. If a pass does not require the tagged operation, those uses are reading the wrong quantity. The testing standards treat that score use as a claim that must be validated for the intended interpretation~\cite{aera2014standards}.

That inference is an argument, not a definition~\cite{Kane_1992,Kane_2013,Messick_1995}. It can be wrong even when the publisher's tag is sincere. The item may be answerable from a surface cue, from the choices without the stem, or from a formula that never binds symbols to the situation the standard names.

This paper searches for those rival explanations as programs. A program that lacks an operation the tag names, never sees the published key, and still matches the key under the published scoring rule is a \emph{witness}. A witness refutes the claim that a pass on that item requires the tagged operation. A search that finds no witness does not certify the tag beyond the programs that were tried. We do not estimate whether students learned the skill, whether humans use the shortcuts, or which curriculum is better. We never say students do not learn~$X$.

Results are cell-level pass rates for named programs, not an item-by-operation table. Catalog rules lack content operations. Masked-stem withholds the given quantities. TIMSS items keep knowing, applying, and reasoning. We do not assign CCSS codes to TIMSS items.

Our contributions are the object, the bar, the census, and what the census found.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A one-sided test of a tag that needs no student responses.} We turn the claim that an item requires an operation into a falsifiable statement about programs: a program that lacks the tagged operation, never sees the published key, and matches the key under the published scoring rule is a witness that a pass does not require that operation. The test uses no student data and never yields a claim that students failed to learn. Validity theory states the argument; cognitive diagnosis and test-wiseness research estimate students or list cues. Neither scores this object (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
\item \textbf{A pre-registered witness bar and family discipline.} A witness must fire on at least 10 items with a lower 95\% confidence bound above format chance, adjusted for option elimination; searched and fitted programs must also beat the 95th percentile of random programs of matched complexity. Programs are hashed before scoring. Catalog rules, adaptive strategies, and partial-input language models are three families, each Holm-corrected on its own, so a row that beats chance in one cell is never billed as a family-wise discovery. Every claimed row must also clear its bar on the items whose PDF text layer matches the printed page, checked mechanically against a transcription of the page image under pre-registered thresholds. Before any null is read, the same searcher must recover published shallow-cue rates on public benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{A census of tagged public-release mathematics.} We assemble 2{,}405 keyed items from nine authorities, each tag quoted from its authority, including the last TIMSS cycle whose items, keys, and domain tags are public together. Channels are defined by the information and operations a program lacks: surface cues, adaptive test-taking strategies, substitution of the options into the printed equation read from the page image, and language models that see the question with every given quantity withheld.
\item \textbf{What the census found.} The item-writing catalog yields no family-wise discovery. Two rows beat chance in their cell on the text layer; when the text layer is checked against the printed page, one (EQAO grade~6, 14 faithful items) still clears and awaits replication, and the other (New York Regents geometry) does not and is not claimed. The one strategy that survived correction failed a powered replication. A language model in two model families recovers New York Regents Algebra~I keys with the given quantities withheld, on print-faithful items; the same row on Algebra~II clears on the text layer and fails on print-faithful items, so it is not claimed. The Algebra~I keys it recovers are on items whose publisher's tag names recognizing or interpreting a form; on items whose tag names execution on the givens, no model clears. So on those matched items a pass does not require the given quantities, which is a narrower statement than a pass omitting the tagged operation. Image-channel backsolving fires on a small unique-equation subset of Algebra~I. TIMSS knowing, applying, and reasoning stay null at the public $n$ of about 50 to 80, a power-limited non-refutation, with the same searcher validated on benchmarks with documented cues (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

This census uses public-release items. TIMSS 2011 is the last cycle whose item texts, keys, scoring guides, domain tags, and percent correct are public together, with no permission process. From 2015, the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) keeps most items secured; restricted-use grants typically cap example items and forbid the comparisons this census needs. Recency of showable items comes from released state and provincial tests, not from a later TIMSS cycle.
