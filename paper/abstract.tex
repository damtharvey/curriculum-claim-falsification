Assessment items carry tags, standard codes, cognitive domains, or
rubric criteria, and accountability, procurement, and research read a
correct answer as evidence of the tagged competency, which assumes
passing requires it. We test that assumption without student data by
searching for assessment bypasses: solvers that lack the tagged
competency by an explicit restriction on operations or information,
never see the key, and pass under the published scoring rule. A bypass
shows passing does not require what was denied; it says nothing about
students, and finding none does not certify a tag.

We instantiate the search on public-release mathematics items, where
tags, keys, and scoring rules are public, under a pre-registered bar.
On New York Regents Algebra~I and II, language models of two families
recover the key well above chance with every given quantity withheld,
and the recovery survives a form-preserving change of every option
value. A pass there does not require computing on the givens, so the
key does not carry the evidence it is read as; whether it requires the
tagged operation is left open. Small programs denied the tagged
operation match keys on a few cells. Trends in International
Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) cognitive-domain cells yield no
bypass at public sizes. Rates are claimed on items re-read from the
printed page, which released text layers often misstate. This is a
method demonstration on one subject; the search needs only public tags,
item text, keys, and an executable scoring rule.
