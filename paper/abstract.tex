Released mathematics items carry a published tag: a curriculum
standard, or a cognitive domain such as reasoning. A correct answer is
then read as evidence of that competency in accountability and
research. If a pass does not require the tagged operation, those uses
are reading the wrong quantity. We ask whether a program lacking that
operation can match the published key. A match is one-sided: the pass
does not require the tagged operation; it does not say students failed
to learn. Not finding one does not certify the tag.

On public-release items from nine authorities we score three program
families, each Holm-corrected for multiple testing on its own:
item-writing rules, test-taking strategies, and language models shown
the question with its given quantities withheld. The text layer of a
released PDF often differs from the print, so claims are made only on
items whose text matches the page; two rows that cleared on the text
layer fail on those items. The item-writing rules yield no family-wise
discovery; one beats chance on one cell and awaits replication.
Language models of two families recover New York Regents Algebra~I
keys with the given quantities withheld, on items tagged for
recognizing or interpreting a form; there a pass does not require
those quantities. No program clears the Trends in
International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) knowing, applying,
or reasoning cells, each about 50 to 80 public items.
