Released mathematics items carry a published tag, a standard code or a
cognitive domain, and a correct answer is read as evidence of that
competency. If a pass does not require the tagged operation, that
reading is of the wrong quantity. We ask whether a program lacking the
operation can match the published key. A match is one-sided: it does
not say students failed to learn, and finding none does not certify
the tag.

On released items from nine authorities we score three Holm-corrected
program families: item-writing rules, test-taking strategies, and
language models shown the question with its given quantities withheld.
The text layer of a released PDF often differs from the print, so
claimed rows are measured on items re-read from the page and, for the
language models, confirmed by a second independent transcription. The
item-writing rules yield no family-wise discovery; one beats chance on
two cells and awaits replication. Language models of two families
recover New York Regents Algebra~I and Algebra~II keys, and the
recovery survives a change of every option value with the form kept.
On items that two independent codings of item demand mark as requiring
computation on the givens, one of three scorers misses the bar; so on
the matched items a pass does not require the given quantities, which
is narrower than omitting the tagged operation. The international
cognitive-domain cells, at about 50 to 80 public items each, yield no
such program.
