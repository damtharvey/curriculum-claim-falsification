Released mathematics items carry a published tag, a standard code or a
cognitive domain, and a correct answer is read as evidence of the tagged
competency. That reading assumes passing requires the competency. We
test the assumption on public-release items, without student data, by
searching for assessment bypasses: solvers that lack the tagged
competency by an explicit restriction on operations or information,
never see the key, and still pass under the published scoring rule. A
bypass shows that passing does not require what was denied; it says
nothing about students, and finding none does not certify a tag.

Under a pre-registered bar we find bypasses. On New York Regents
Algebra~I and Algebra~II, a language model shown the question with every
given quantity withheld recovers the key well above chance in two model
families, and the recovery survives a change of every option value with
the form kept. A pass there does not require computing on the givens, so
the key does not carry the evidence it is read as; whether it requires
the tagged operation is left open. Small programs denied the tagged
operation match keys on a few cells. The Trends in International
Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) cognitive-domain cells yield no
bypass at their public sizes. Claimed rates are measured on items
re-read from the printed page, which released text layers often
misstate.
